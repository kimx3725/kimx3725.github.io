Dongmin (Dennis) Kim

dongmin_kim [at] g [dot] harvard [dot] edu

I am a quantitative ecologist interested in applying statistical tools to infer behavioural processes from empirical data. I am also the lead data curator for the MoveDisease Archive, a global data platform linking animal movement data with diagnostic and health data from wildlife systems worldwide. My research focuses on understanding how behavioural processes vary within and among populations and species in response to individual heterogeneity and zoonotic disease.

Currently, I am a George E. Burch Postdoctoral Fellow at the Smithsonian Conservation Biology Institute. Previously, I was a postdoctoral associate in Jesús Pinto-Ledezma's lab at the University of Minnesota and Paul Moorcroft's lab at Harvard University. I received my PhD at the University of Minnesota in 2024, where I was co-advised by Allison Shaw and John Fieberg. Before my PhD, I studied statistics at the University of Minnesota, Morris.

A little blurb about me

I tend to see myself as a quantitative ecologist (about 60%) and a statistical ecologist (about 40%). As Ruth King mentioned in her talk at the BioSS Annual Meeting 2021, “Quantitative Ecology” is a more ecology-driven thought process that focuses on applying advanced mathematical and statistical methods to ecology. In contrast, “Statistical Ecology” focuses more on developing new methodologies for analyzing ecological data than on applying them.

When I think of Quantitative Ecology, I always think of two distinct sub-fields: theoretical (mathematical) and statistical applications. Fortunately, I was exposed to both worlds through two wonderful PhD advisors, Allison Shaw, a theoretical ecologist, and John Fieberg, a statistical ecologist, who showed me what it means to be a quantitative ecologist.

During my PhD, I learned how different these two perspectives are, and how each helps me develop and apply methods in movement ecology. In theoretical ecology, you build your own world with mathematical formulas to ask under what conditions we would see the ecological phenomena we observe in nature. With Allison, I got to play with ordinary differential equations to understand how partial migration emerges in parasite-migratory host systems. We also allowed for different host defense mechanisms, each with associated trade-offs: here, we incorporated the reproductive costs of becoming tolerant and/or migratory to reduce the costs of infection (Kim et al. 2021; Journal of Animal Ecology). Allison, the lab members, and friends (myself included) also put together a guideline of how each ecologist has their own persona for building worlds with mathematical formulas, showing that developing a theoretical model is not as complex or scary as it seems (Shaw et al. 2025; Proc B).

In statistical ecology, you develop and apply statistical methods to understand associations between factors that may influence ecological processes. With John, I got to learn habitat selection models (Step Selection Functions (SSFs), in particular!) and how to parameterize familiarity (or memory) as an additional function within the SSF framework (Kim et al. 2024; Movement Ecology). I also worked on applying different formulations of hidden Markov models to movement data to infer disease progression in free-roaming animals, in collaboration with Théo Michelot and Smithsonian researchers Jared Stabach and Katherine Mertes (Kim et al. 2026; Journal of Applied Ecology).

So, going back to what I wanted to say... I love solving problems that require a quantitative solution. I love this quote from W. Edwards Deming, which my PhD advisor John put on his lab website: “In God we trust; all others must bring data.” Okay, I’ve talked too much! So what are my research interests? See below.

Research interests

  • Animal movement models
  • Habitat selection models
  • Hidden (semi-) Markov models and their applications to disease ecology
  • Bayesian inference
  • Movement ecology
  • Disease ecology
  • Cognitive ecology
  • Data fusion (what data may be useful for movement and disease ecology; MoveDisease Archive)
